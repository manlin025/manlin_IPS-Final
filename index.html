Too Early

In 1988, a few missing words in a telegram sent my father to Wuxi one day too early.

Telegram Message · 1988

Liang — 22 — to Wuxi

My father was born in 1969, into a world where distance was measured not only in miles, but also in how long it took a message to arrive.

The summer of 1988, shortly after he was accepted into college, his father gave him some money and sent him on a journey to Wuxi, his hometown in eastern China, to visit relatives. Growing up in Beijing at a time when photographs were rare and precious, my father drew a blank when he thought about Wuxi.

Before he left, his father had to tell the family when to meet him. A letter was too slow, and there were no cell phones or internet — the quickest choice was a telegram.

But telegrams charged by the Chinese character. Every word had a price, so people learned to make their message as short as possible. His father reduced the entire journey to a few characters: “Liang — 22 — to Wuxi.”

What he meant was: Liang will arrive in Wuxi on the 22nd. What the family understood was: Liang will leave for Wuxi on the 22nd.

The difference was only a few missing words — and one entire day.

When my father stepped off the train on the 22nd, no one was there. He searched the station for a familiar face, but the crowd moved around him without stopping. Somewhere in Wuxi, his relatives were still expecting him tomorrow.

There was no message he could send to correct the mistake. No blue dot on a map could show him where he was. No search bar could tell him where to go. In his pocket was only a small piece of paper with an address on it.

So he began to ask.

He carried his suitcase through the unfamiliar city, showed the paper to one stranger and another. Each person gave him a small part of the way. The address slowly became a real place. By following a trail of strangers’ voices, he eventually reached his family’s door.

A few missing words in a telegram had left him alone at the station, but his journey was not over.

Later, he took a boat from Wuxi to Hangzhou, traveling along the canal. In Hangzhou, he planned to visit one of his father’s old friends. He had been told that the man worked at a television station, so after getting off the boat, he dragged his suitcase all the way there.

When he arrived, the people at the station gave him unexpected news: the man had stopped working there a long time ago.

My father could hardly believe it. Once again, he had reached the place he was given, only to find that the person he was looking for was somewhere else.

Fortunately, someone at the station knew the man’s home address. My father took a bus across the city, carrying his suitcase with him, and eventually found the house.

Before the internet, a journey depended on small pieces of information: a telegram shortened to save money, an address written on paper, or a stranger pointing down an unfamiliar street. If any part of that information was unclear or outdated, a traveler could easily be left alone.

My father had no phone, no digital map, and no way to announce that he had arrived. He had only his suitcase, a paper address, and the patience to keep asking for directions. Somehow, that was enough.
