One Day Too Early

Background
Knowledge

A little context for a journey made before smartphones, digital maps, and instant messages.

China in 1988

In 1988, China was changing quickly, but everyday communication was still slow and limited. The internet was not part of ordinary life, and personal mobile phones were not something most families could use. If someone left home, their family could not simply call or text.

Communication before the internet

People depended on letters, landline telephones, telegrams, and word of mouth. A letter could take days to arrive. A phone call usually required both people to be near a telephone at the same time, and long-distance calls could be difficult or expensive.

When a message was urgent, people often used a telegram. Telegrams were delivered on paper and charged by the number of Chinese characters. To save money, senders removed anything that seemed unnecessary, sometimes including the very words that made a message clear.

Traveling with fragments of information

Without online maps, a traveler had to prepare before leaving. A handwritten address or directions from a relative could be the only clues they carried. If the information was incomplete, there was no quick way to verify it.

Finding a place often meant asking strangers along the way: at a station, on a bus, or on the street. Each answer could provide only one small part of the route.

That is why a telegram such as “Liang — 22 — to Wuxi” matters in this story. Its brevity was practical, but it also left room for two different interpretations: is Liang arriving on the 22nd, or leaving on the 22nd?

Why this story feels different today

Today, a missed meeting can be fixed with a quick text, a shared location, or a search. In 1988, a misunderstanding could leave someone alone in an unfamiliar city with only a suitcase, a paper address, and the willingness to keep asking. The journey in One Day Too Early is about that gap--and about the strangers who helped bridge it.
